> **My decision:** I walked away from a job that was, on paper, very close to what I had been working towards. Not because it was bad. Not because I was afraid of the challenge. I left because the opportunity arrived at the wrong time, demanded energy from things I was no longer willing to sacrifice, and showed me that I wanted the destination more than I wanted the everyday work required to get there.

For quite a while, I thought I knew what the next stage of my career should look like.

Healthcare.

Regional scope.

Proper Finance Business Partnering.

Messy problems.

Multiple countries.

Enough responsibility to eventually move towards CFO-type work.

Good salary.

Good boss.

Flexibility.

Then I got something remarkably close to it.

And roughly two months later, I resigned.

Even writing that sounds stupid.

Because the job was not bad.

Quite the opposite.

That is what made leaving **SO** difficult.

## On paper, I should have stayed

There were plenty of reasons to.

I genuinely wanted to work in healthcare.

The role had regional scope and enough complexity to keep me interested.

It moved me further along the Finance Business Partner route I thought I wanted.

The salary was good.

My bosses were good.

They were supportive.

They understood that I had a newborn daughter and a young son.

They gave me flexibility.

Nobody was standing over me demanding that I choose work over family.

So let me make something very clear.

**I did not leave because the job was too difficult.**

I actually like difficult.

Messy situations energise me.

Give me something unclear, broken or complicated and I will usually start thinking about how to fix it.

I have never wanted an easy job simply so I can coast.

The problem was different.

The job was asking me a question I could no longer avoid:

**Even if I can do this, do I actually want to spend this part of my life doing it?**

## I didn't want to choose between professionalism and family

I take work quite seriously.

Maybe too seriously sometimes.

If I accept a regional role with meaningful responsibility, I want to do it properly.

I don't want to be the finance person who is perpetually saying:

Sorry, child sick.

Sorry, need to leave.

Sorry, cannot finish.

Sorry, family issue.

My bosses might be completely understanding.

That is almost beside the point.

**I know the standard I expect from myself.**

If somebody trusts me with an important role, I want to deserve the trust.

I want the work to be good.

I want to be dependable.

I want the people around me to know that when I say I will do something, it gets done.

And then I go home.

Newborn daughter.

Young son.

One child needs something.

Then the other needs something.

Someone falls sick.

Someone does not sleep.

My wife needs support.

My son wants Papa.

My daughter is only going to be this tiny once.

There is only so much energy in one human being.

Eventually something has to give.

And I realised I hated both available versions of myself.

One version gave the job everything and came home depleted.

The other protected the family but constantly felt that I was giving work less than what the role deserved.

I did not want to become either.

## Could I have simply lowered my standards?

Probably.

This is one of the harder questions I asked myself.

Maybe the problem was me.

Maybe I did not need to operate at 100%.

Maybe 80% professionally was perfectly acceptable for a while.

My bosses had already given me flexibility.

Perhaps I could have stopped expecting so much from myself and made the arrangement work.

That is a fair criticism.

And maybe some people would have done exactly that.

But I know myself.

I would still see the unfinished work.

I would still know what I could have done better.

I would still carry it home in my head.

And if the solution to keeping the job was spending the next few years feeling mediocre at work and guilty at home, I wasn't sure what exactly I was preserving.

A salary?

A title?

A career path?

All useful.

But useful for what?

## Maybe the opportunity arrived in the wrong season

My daughter had just been born.

I barely had what I would consider a proper paternity period.

My son was already sharing more of me than he ever had before.

And it wasn't just about the children.

For the first time since Liam was born, my wife finally had a chance to slow down a little during maternity.

I missed her dearly.

The last couple of years had been pregnancy, work, childcare, another pregnancy, another newborn, and both of us constantly moving.

I wanted some of this time with her too.

Not as two people managing the next task.

Just as husband and wife, while life had briefly given us a little more space.

And this thought kept bothering me:

**Their childhood is irreversible.**

I can earn another dollar.

I can rebuild a career.

I can find another difficult job.

Maybe another regional job.

Maybe another healthcare opportunity.

Maybe something even better.

There are no guarantees, obviously.

I may never get exactly the same opportunity again.

That is the risk I took.

But I know something with absolute certainty.

My son will never be this age again.

My daughter will never be a newborn again.

There is no recruiter who can call me ten years from now and say:

> Good news. We found you another six months with your baby.

That job does not exist.

Time moves whether I am ready or not.

And once I saw the decision that way, the trade-off stopped looking equal.

## But family was not the whole reason

This is the uncomfortable part.

If I absolutely loved the work, maybe I would have fought harder to make it work.

I didn't.

I liked the industry.

I liked the challenge.

I liked talking to the business.

I liked regional complexity.

I liked understanding why something happened and helping people decide what to do next.

But there was still a lot of reporting.

Recurring reports.

Budget cycles.

Variance explanations.

Numbers produced because numbers needed to be produced.

That work drains me.

Not because it is beneath me.

Not because it is easy.

I simply don't enjoy it enough.

I would rather research.

Think.

Plan.

Write.

Solve ambiguous problems.

Build things.

Help somebody make a difficult decision.

Work on something where the answer is not already hiding inside a reporting template.

And slowly I had to admit something to myself:

**I may have wanted the destination more than I wanted the road.**

Healthcare.

Regional FBP.

CFO path.

Those words looked right.

But careers are not lived as job descriptions.

They are lived on random Tuesdays.

What do you actually do at 10:30am?

What do you spend three hours fixing?

What task are you still thinking about at night?

What will you do again next month?

And next quarter?

And next year?

That is the career.

Not the title.

Maybe I liked the idea of where the role could eventually lead more than I liked enough of the work required to get there.

That mattered.

## Was two months enough?

Probably not, if the objective was to produce a fair review of the company or the role.

But I wasn't writing a performance review.

I was making a decision about my life.

Could things have improved after six months?

Of course.

Could I have become more efficient?

Yes.

Could reporting have become less painful once I understood the systems?

Probably.

Could the role have evolved towards more business partnering over time?

Very possibly.

That is why I cannot say:

> This career path is wrong.

I don't know that.

I can only say:

> **I knew enough to realise I did not want to spend the next year finding out at the expense of this particular year with my family.**

That is a different threshold.

Sometimes we stay because we need certainty that leaving is correct.

But certainty has a cost too.

Another six months.

Another year.

Another child growing.

Sometimes the decision is not:

> Do I know this is wrong?

It is:

> **Do I know enough that I am no longer willing to keep paying for more information?**

For me, yes.

## Am I underestimating how hard it will be to come back?

Maybe.

This is another risk I don't want to romanticise.

Career momentum is real.

Salary progression is real.

Recruiters care about recent experience.

Companies may ask why I left.

The market may not conveniently wait until I decide I am ready again.

And perhaps several years from now I will look back and think:

> Wah. That was a damn good opportunity. Why did I let it go?

Possible.

I am accepting that.

A Decision is not good because it has no downside.

Sometimes the downside is the price.

I gave up income.

Momentum.

A credible path.

A role that many people would probably tell me to hold onto.

I did not walk away believing those things were worthless.

**I walked away knowing exactly what they were worth and deciding something else was worth more right now.**

## This is a season, not a retirement speech

I am still ambitious.

I still care about money.

I still like difficult problems.

I still want to build things.

And I suspect there will be another period in my life when I push very hard professionally again.

Maybe sooner than I expect.

Maybe differently.

What changed is not that career became meaningless.

It moved down the list.

For now.

There is a difference between abandoning ambition and deciding when to deploy it.

Right now, I want more autonomy.

More time.

More creative energy.

More time with the children.

Enough space to think about what I actually want to build next instead of automatically accepting the next rung because it is there.

Maybe I accelerate again later.

Maybe the next path looks nothing like the one I just left.

I don't know.

That uncertainty scares me a little.

It also feels strangely honest.

## When two good things compete, ask which one comes back

| What I could lose                   | If I leave                            | Can I get it back?                          |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Salary                              | Give up a good salary                 | **Probably. Earn again.**                   |
| Career momentum                     | Pause a strong regional FBP/CFO path  | **Probably. Rebuild.**                      |
| Healthcare opportunity              | Leave an industry I wanted            | **Maybe. Other opportunities may come.**    |
| Good bosses                         | Walk away from supportive leadership  | **Maybe. Not guaranteed.**                  |
| Challenge                           | Lose a difficult, energising role     | **Yes. Difficult problems are everywhere.** |
| **Liam at this age**                | —                                     | **NO.**                                     |
| **Ada as a newborn**                | —                                     | **NO.**                                     |
| **This exact season of our family** | —                                     | **NO.**                                     |

This is probably the thing that finally made the decision possible.

The choice was not:

Good job versus bad job.

It was:

**Two valuable things competing for the same finite time.**

Professionalism.

Money.

Career momentum.

Family.

Autonomy.

Energy.

I wanted all of them.

I could not maximise all of them.

So I asked myself which losses could potentially be repaired later.

Money?

Earn again.

Career?

Rebuild.

Opportunity?

Maybe another one comes.

Reputation?

Work for it again.

But time with a two-year-old?

Gone.

The newborn stage?

Gone.

The version of my family that exists today?

Gone too.

Not tragically.

Just normally.

Children grow.

That is what they are supposed to do.

Which is precisely why the time matters.

I got remarkably close to the job I thought I wanted.

Then I discovered that getting what you want can sometimes teach you that the thing itself was never the whole point.

The job was good.

The bosses were good.

The opportunity was real.

Maybe in another season I would have stayed.

This time, I left.

I can earn the money again.

I can find another challenge.

I can rebuild the career.

**But childhood doesn't wait for me to be ready.**
